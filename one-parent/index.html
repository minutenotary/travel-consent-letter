One parent on the trip

Travelling with one parent: the letter from the one at home

It is the most common consent letter there is. One parent takes the children abroad, the other stays in Canada and puts their agreement in writing. The rule is short; the trips are not. Here are the versions families actually take.

Book with Minute Notary

In four lines

  • The parent who stays home gives the consent and signs.
  • The parent who travels is named as the adult with the child.
  • One letter per trip, with the dates and places of that trip.
  • Canada does not require it by law; officers and airlines may still ask.

Signing it before a notary, or declaring it before a commissioner, gives an officer a name and a seal to check.

When the last names do not match

A mother travelling with a child who carries the father's surname is the situation officers question most often, and the letter alone does not explain it. Pack a document that shows both of you as parent and child.

  • For a child born in Ontario: the birth certificate that lists the parents. ServiceOntario issues a short certificate without parents' names and a version with them; only the second one helps here.
  • After a name change: the change of name certificate, for you or for the child.
  • After adoption: the adoption order or the amended birth record.

Carry originals, or copies a notary has certified after seeing the originals in person.

Two ways to sign it with us

At your door

We send a notary to the parent who stays home, wherever they are in Ontario. The notary matches their photo ID to the letter, watches the signature and adds a notarial certificate with the date and seal. Leave the signature line empty until the notary is there.

As a declaration, over live video

The parent at home can make the letter as a solemn declaration before a commissioner by secure video, from anywhere in Ontario. The wording becomes a declaration of consent, and the commissioner's block records that it was taken remotely under Ontario Regulation 431/20. This route suits a parent who is at work or away from home that week, anywhere in the province.

Either way, the notary confirms who signed and when. Choosing who should consent, or reading a parenting order, belongs to the parents and, where needed, a family lawyer.

Questions people ask

Does the parent who is travelling sign the letter too?
No. The travelling parent is the adult named in the letter. Consent comes from the parent who is not on the trip.
Is one letter enough for every trip this year?
Write a new letter for each trip. A letter tied to the dates and places of one trip is what border officers expect to see; an open letter for any travel invites questions.
Is a consent letter required by law in Canada?
Canadian law does not require one. Canada recommends it, border officers on either side may ask for it, and some airlines and countries set their own rules, so check with the airline and the destination country before you fly.
What if the other parent will not sign?
A notary cannot replace a missing consent. Speak with a family lawyer before the trip.
